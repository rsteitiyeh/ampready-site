"Journeyman electrician classes" means two different things depending on where you are in the process. If you haven't started an apprenticeship yet, it means the related classroom instruction built into a registered apprenticeship program. If you've already logged your hours and are getting ready for the state exam, it means a standalone exam-prep course. Picking the wrong one wastes money and time, so figure out which stage you're in before you compare options.

Both paths lead to the same license, but they're not interchangeable and they're priced completely differently. Apprenticeship classroom hours are usually part of a package deal with your on-the-job training. Exam-prep courses are a separate purchase you make once your hours are already done.

## Am I choosing apprenticeship classes or exam-prep classes?

If you're not yet working under a licensed electrician logging hours, you need an apprenticeship program, not an exam-prep course. If you're already close to or past your hour requirement, you need exam prep, not another apprenticeship.

A registered electrician apprenticeship runs about 4 years with roughly 8,000 hours of on-the-job training plus related classroom instruction, per the [U.S. Department of Labor's apprenticeship standard for the electrician occupation](https://www.apprenticeship.gov/apprenticeship-occupations/listings?occupationCode=47-2111.00). The classroom portion of that program is what most people mean by "apprenticeship classes." Exam-prep classes are different: shorter, standalone courses aimed at candidates who already have their hours and just need to drill code navigation and calculations before test day.

## How do union and non-union apprenticeship classes compare?

Union apprenticeship classes through the Electrical Training Alliance (formerly NJATC, tied to the IBEW) charge no tuition. Non-union merit-shop programs through IEC or ABC follow the same federal hour standard but can carry tuition or book costs depending on the chapter and employer.

The Electrical Training Alliance states plainly that apprentices "earn while they learn... without cost to the taxpayers," meaning no tuition is charged to the apprentice, according to [electricaltrainingalliance.org](https://www.electricaltrainingalliance.org/AboutUs). IEC's national site confirms its non-union programs run "about four years... a minimum of 8,000 hours of OJT and 576 hours of classroom instruction," and notes that tuition assistance may come from the sponsoring employer while students often cover their own books, per [IEC's apprenticeship page](https://www.ieci.org/apprenticeship). That's the real difference to budget for: union programs are free to the apprentice by design, and non-union programs can carry real out-of-pocket cost that varies chapter to chapter.

## What actually matters when choosing an exam-prep class?

Match the course to your state's actual exam vendor and current code edition, check who's teaching it, and get the total cost in writing before you enroll.

| What to check | Why it matters | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Exam vendor alignment | Your state uses either PSI or Pearson VUE, and the format differs (section splits, time limits, question counts) | Course doesn't mention which vendor or state it's built for |
| NEC edition | States lag the current NFPA edition by a code cycle or more; Texas tests 2023 NEC, Washington tests 2020 NEC as of this writing | Course doesn't specify which NEC edition it teaches to |
| Instructor background | No state licenses or approves exam-prep instructors directly, so credentials are the provider's own claim, not a regulated standard | Vague "certified expert" language with no name or license number |
| Total cost | Confirmed real-world pricing runs from a few hundred dollars (community college or trade-association courses) to over a thousand for tiered video/book programs | Price only revealed after you provide contact info |
| Pass-rate claims | No state publishes an approved list of exam-prep providers, so "our students pass at 95%" numbers are unverifiable self-reporting | A specific pass-rate stat with no methodology or source behind it |

We checked pricing directly where providers publish it: Mike Holt Enterprises sells tiered journeyman exam-prep libraries from $400 for a basic package up to $1,599 for its most complete tier, per [mikeholt.com](https://www.mikeholt.com/examprep-journeyman.php). Delgado Community College in Louisiana runs a journeyman prep course, ELEC 210, that's tuition-free beyond the application fee, per [dcc.edu](https://www.dcc.edu). CITC in Washington state runs a 3-day in-person journeyman exam-prep course for $600, per [ceu.citcwa.org](https://ceu.citcwa.org). That spread, tuition-free to $1,599, is real, and it tells you cost alone doesn't indicate quality. Match the course to your state and code edition first, then compare price among options that actually fit.

## Does any state publish an approved list of exam-prep providers?

No. What some states publish is a list of approved continuing-education providers for license renewal, which is a different, separate program from exam prep, and the two get confused constantly.

Texas TDLR, for example, maintains a [continuing education provider list](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/electricians/elecce.htm), but that list governs the CE hours you need to renew an already-issued license, not classes to prepare for your original journeyman exam. There is no equivalent state-approved list for exam-prep courses themselves, which means "state-approved" claims on a prep course's marketing page aren't backed by any actual state approval process. Treat that phrase as a marketing claim, not a credential, unless the provider can point to the specific state program it refers to.

## Should I take an in-person or online journeyman class?

Both are legitimate, and the right choice depends on whether you learn better with a fixed schedule and in-room accountability or with flexible, self-paced review.

In-person courses, like CITC's 3-day format, compress prep into a fixed block with a live instructor to ask questions of in real time. Online and video-based options, like Mike Holt's tiered libraries, let you work around a job site schedule and revisit specific topics as many times as you need. Neither format changes what the exam tests. ## FAQ

**What's the difference between apprenticeship classes and journeyman exam-prep classes?**
Apprenticeship classes are the required classroom instruction built into a registered apprenticeship program, tied to your on-the-job training hours. Exam-prep classes are standalone courses taken once your hours are complete, focused specifically on passing the state licensing exam.

**Are union journeyman apprenticeship classes free?**
Yes, in the sense that the Electrical Training Alliance's union apprenticeship programs charge no tuition to the apprentice. Non-union programs through IEC or ABC follow the same federal hour standard but can carry tuition or book costs depending on the chapter.

**How much do journeyman electrician exam-prep classes cost?**
It ranges widely. We found tuition-free community college options (application fee only), a 3-day in-person course at $600, and tiered online video/book programs from $400 to $1,599. Cost doesn't indicate quality; matching the course to your state's exam vendor and NEC edition does.

**Are online journeyman electrician classes as good as in-person classes?**
Both are legitimate options. Online classes offer flexibility around a work schedule; in-person classes offer a fixed timeline and live instructor access. Neither format changes the content the exam actually tests.

**Does any state approve or certify journeyman exam-prep courses?**
No. States publish approved-provider lists for continuing education used in license renewal, a separate program from original exam prep. No state maintains an approved list for exam-prep courses themselves, so "state-approved" marketing claims on a prep course should be verified directly, not taken at face value.
