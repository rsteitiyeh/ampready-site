Studying for the master electrician exam means treating it as two separate tests, not one. Most states split the exam into a code-knowledge portion and a calculations portion, each scored and passed independently, so your study plan needs a code-navigation track and a math track that get built and practiced separately, not blended into one generic review.

How much of each track you need depends on your state. Some states, like Texas, test pure NEC knowledge and calculations with no business content. Others fold a business and law section, or state-specific regulatory rules, into the same exam. Before you build a study calendar, confirm exactly what your state tests.

## What does the master electrician exam actually test?

It tests code knowledge and applied calculations at a harder level than the journeyman exam, and in some states it adds a business or regulatory component on top.

Texas is a clean worked example because TDLR publishes exact numbers. The Texas master electrician exam is split into an NEC Knowledge portion (75 items, 5 unscored, 150 minutes) and a Calculations portion (33 items, 3 unscored, 170 minutes), each requiring a 70% passing score on its own, according to [TDLR's Candidate Information Bulletin](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/electricians/pdf/Candidate-Information-Bulletin.pdf). There's no business or law section in the Texas outline: the content categories run from definitions and theory through services, feeders, branch circuits, wiring methods, motors and generators, special occupancies, and renewable energy, all technical. Virginia runs a single 90-item, 270-minute master exam requiring 63 correct (70%) to pass, but its content outline blends technical NEC material with state regulatory sections covering standards of practice, standards of conduct, and license renewal rules, per [PSI's Virginia Candidate Information Bulletin](https://test-takers.psiexams.com/api/content/bulletin/476). Florida and North Carolina go further and test business content directly (see the table below).

## How many hours of experience do I need before I can sit for the exam?

In most states you need a set number of years as a licensed journeyman plus documented on-the-job hours, and the exact numbers are state-specific.

Texas requires at least 2 years as a licensed journeyman electrician and 12,000 total hours of on-the-job training under a licensed master electrician before you're eligible to apply, according to [TDLR's master electrician application page](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/electricians/apply/individuals/master-electrician.htm). That's 4,000 hours more than the 8,000 OJT hours required to earn the journeyman license in the first place, so the jump from journeyman to master isn't a quick add-on: it's roughly half again as much documented field time. For comparison, the U.S. Department of Labor's registered apprenticeship standard for the trade runs 4 years and about 8,000 OJT hours plus related classroom instruction, per [apprenticeship.gov's electrician occupation listing](https://www.apprenticeship.gov/apprenticeship-occupations/listings?occupationCode=47-2111.00), so the master-level hour requirement in a state like Texas sits on top of, not instead of, that baseline.

## How does the master exam differ by state?

The biggest split is between states that layer a business and law section onto the master exam and states that don't, plus which NEC edition each one currently tests.

| State | Credential | Experience required | Business/law tested | NEC edition tested |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Texas | Master Electrician | 2 yrs as journeyman + 12,000 total OJT hours | No | 2023 NEC |
| Florida | Certified Electrical Contractor | No statewide journeyman/master ladder; licensed as a contractor through DBPR | Yes, separate 50-question Business Practices exam, 75% to pass | 2023 NEC (moving to 2026 NEC effective August 21, 2027) |
| North Carolina | Unlimited Electrical Contractor | Roughly 8,000 to 9,000 hours for the Unlimited classification | Yes, built into the exam via the NASCLA Contractors Guide to Business, Law and Project Management | 2020 NEC currently enforced (2023 NEC adopted but under legislative delay) |
| Virginia | Master Electrician | Journeyman license plus documented field experience | Yes, state Standards of Practice and Conduct sections | Not clearly confirmed on the current bulletin; verify directly with DPOR before test day |

Sources: [TDLR](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/electricians/pdf/Candidate-Information-Bulletin.pdf), [Florida DBPR reference list](https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/servop/testing/documents/Elec.References.List.pdf), [NCBEEC exam page](https://www.ncbeec.org/examinations/), [Virginia DPOR](https://www.dpor.virginia.gov/Boards/Tradesmen).

Florida is worth calling out specifically because it breaks the pattern candidates often assume: there's no statewide journeyman-to-master ladder at all. The state issues a Certified Electrical Contractor credential through the Department of Business and Professional Regulation, and actual journeyman and master trade licensing happens at the county level in Florida's 67 counties, each running its own hour requirements and exam. If you're studying for "the master exam" in Florida, you first need to know whether you're studying for the state contractor exam or a specific county's trade license.

## How should I structure my study time?

Split your calendar into a code-navigation block and a calculation block, then add a business/law block only if your state tests one, and finish with timed practice under real exam conditions.

1. **Confirm your state's exact structure first.** Get the current candidate bulletin from your board or PSI/Pearson VUE and note the section count, time limits, and whether a business/law section applies. Don't study from a generic national outline.
2. **Get the correct code book edition and tab it properly.** Most exams are open book, but tabbing rules are specific: Texas allows only permanent tabs from the code book's own publisher and bans any handwriting or highlighting during the exam session, per [TDLR's bulletin](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/electricians/pdf/Candidate-Information-Bulletin.pdf). Tab before test day, not during.
3. **Build a code-navigation track.** Drill finding specific articles fast: Article 100 definitions, Article 110 general requirements, and whichever chapters your state's content outline weights heaviest (services, feeders, branch circuits, and special occupancies come up constantly at the master level).
4. **Build a separate calculations track.** Master-level calculation questions tend to combine multiple articles into one scenario, such as a full service load calculation that also touches feeder sizing and overcurrent protection, rather than testing a single formula in isolation. Practice full worked problems, not flashcard-style single-step questions.
5. **Add a business/law block if your state requires it.** In Florida or North Carolina, that means working through the state's actual reference text (Florida's Business Practices content, or North Carolina's NASCLA-based guide) instead of assuming your NEC knowledge covers it.
6. **Run full timed practice sessions in the exam's actual format.** If your state scores sections separately, practice them separately and time each one on its own, not as one combined block.

## Why does prep actually matter here?

Because master-level pass rates are low even among experienced journeymen. In Texas's 2025 fiscal year, the statewide pass rate on the Master NEC Knowledge portion was 22.21% (771 passes out of 3,472 attempts), and the Calculations portion came in even lower at 19.08% (753 out of 3,946), according to [TDLR's own published exam statistics](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/electricians/elecExamStats2025.htm). These are candidates who already hold a journeyman license and logged 12,000 hours of field time. The exam isn't testing whether you can do the work; it's testing whether you can do it fast, under a clock, without your code book bailing you out on speed.

## FAQ

**How do I study for the master electrician exam?**
Confirm your state's exact exam structure first, then build separate study tracks for code navigation and calculations, add a business/law track only if your state tests one, and finish with full timed practice sessions that match your state's real format and time limits.

**How many hours of experience do I need before I can take the master electrician exam?**
It varies by state. Texas requires at least 2 years as a licensed journeyman plus 12,000 total OJT hours. Check your own state board's current requirement, since some states have no statewide master license at all.

**Is the master electrician exam harder than the journeyman exam?**
Generally yes. Texas's 2025 statistics show pass rates under 25% on both master exam portions, and the content typically combines multiple code articles into single scenario questions rather than isolated lookups.

**Can I use my NEC code book during the master exam?**
Usually yes, most states run open-book exams, but tabbing and marking rules are strict. Texas allows only permanent publisher-made tabs and no handwriting or highlighting during the session. Check your own state's rules before test day.

**Does every state test a business and law section on the master exam?**
No. Texas doesn't. Florida, North Carolina, and Virginia each build in some form of business, law, or regulatory content. Confirm which category your state falls into before you plan your study time.
