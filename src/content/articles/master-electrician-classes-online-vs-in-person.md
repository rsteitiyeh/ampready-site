Master electrician classes split into in-person chapter courses run by trade associations and online, self-paced courses you buy once and work through on your own schedule. The real difference isn't quality, it's whether the course bundles in business and law content your state might test, and whether it's built around your specific exam vendor and NEC edition.

Price doesn't track neatly with format either. We found in-person courses under $300 and an online course near $550, so format alone won't tell you which is cheaper. What matters is matching the course to what your state's master exam actually covers.

## What's actually different between online and in-person master classes?

In-person courses run on a fixed schedule with a live instructor in the room; online courses are self-paced and let you revisit material as many times as you want, but neither format changes what's on the exam.

IEC's Fort Worth chapter runs an 8-week Saturday Master Prep course for $225 for members and $275 for non-members, a live-instructor format that forces a consistent weekly study cadence, according to pricing published on the chapter's own registration page. Johnson County Community College in Kansas runs a "Master Electrician Exam Prep" course fully online for $549, giving you the material without a fixed weekly commitment, per [continuinged.jccc.edu](https://continuinged.jccc.edu/certificates/ELEC-002). Neither format inherently covers more content than the other. The real question is whether the specific course, in whichever format, actually matches your state's exam structure.

## Does a master class need to cover business and law content?

Only if your state's exam tests it, and that varies enough that you should confirm before assuming a class covers what you need.

Texas's master exam has no business or law component at all, so a Texas candidate doesn't need that material in a class. Florida tests a separate 50-question Business Practices exam, and North Carolina builds business content directly into its licensing exam through the NASCLA Contractors Guide to Business, Law and Project Management. A class built for a Texas candidate won't prepare a North Carolina or Florida candidate for the business section their state actually requires, and vice versa: a NASCLA-heavy course is more material than a Texas candidate needs. Ask any course directly which state's exam structure it's built around before enrolling, since "master electrician class" doesn't imply one national curriculum.

## Should I pick online or in-person for master-level review?

Pick in-person if you want a fixed weekly deadline and live access to an instructor for calculation questions. Pick online if you're studying around unpredictable job-site hours and want to revisit specific topics repeatedly.

Master-level calculations tend to combine multiple NEC articles into single scenario problems rather than one-step lookups, which is exactly the kind of material that benefits from asking a live instructor "why" in real time, an advantage in-person courses have. Online courses trade that live access for flexibility: you can rewatch a service-calculation walkthrough three times in one sitting if it isn't clicking, something a fixed-schedule in-person class doesn't allow. Neither format substitutes for timed practice under your actual state's exam conditions once the class itself is done.

## FAQ

**Are there master electrician classes available online?**
Yes. Johnson County Community College runs a fully online Master Electrician Exam Prep course for $549. Online options trade live instructor access for schedule flexibility, and cost isn't necessarily higher than in-person alternatives.

**What's the best master electrician exam prep option?**
It depends on your state's exam structure. A course built around a state with no business/law section, like Texas, won't prepare you for Florida's separate Business Practices exam or North Carolina's NASCLA-based content. Match the course to your state first, then compare format and price.

**Is a master electrician training course required to sit for the exam?**
No state requires a specific training course to sit for the master exam. What states require is documented field experience as a licensed journeyman. Classes are optional prep, not a licensing prerequisite.

**How much does a master electrician class cost?**
Confirmed pricing we found ranges from about $225 for an in-person 8-week chapter course up to $575 for a course-plus-exam bundle, with a fully online option around $549. Price varies by chapter and format, not by quality.

**Do master electrician classes cover business and law content?**
Only some do, and only if your state's exam requires it. Confirm directly with the provider which state's exam structure the course is built around before assuming it covers what your state tests.
