Electrician continuing education requirements range from zero hours in states like West Virginia to 32 hours per cycle in California, with everything from a light 3-hour NEC-update requirement in Virginia to Texas's 4-hour annual mandate sitting in between. There's no federal standard here. Every state sets its own hour count, topics, and delivery rules independently.

That range is wide enough that assuming your state follows a "typical" requirement is a real risk. Some states require zero CE and renew on a fee-only basis. Others require dozens of hours from board-approved providers on specific topics. Confirm your own state's current rule before you plan any study time around it.

## Which states require CE hours, and which don't?

Roughly half the states we checked require CE hours; the rest renew on a fee-only basis with no education requirement attached.

West Virginia's current licensing rule, 103CSR5, effective May 1, 2024, has no CE section for journeyman or master electricians at all, only the renewal fee, per the [WV State Fire Marshal](https://firemarshal.wv.gov/media/39366/download). That's a genuine zero-hour state, not a state with a light requirement. On the other end, California requires 32 hours of CE per 3-year cycle plus 2,000 hours of verified work experience, the heaviest combined requirement of any state checked, according to [California's Department of Industrial Relations](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/ecu/ECU_FAQ.htm). Georgia, which licenses electrical contractors rather than individual journeymen, requires 8 CE hours per 2-year cycle and, starting January 1, 2026, mandates that all CE be logged through the CE Broker platform, per the [Georgia Secretary of State](https://sos.ga.gov/page/electrical-contractor-continuing-education).

## What topics do CE hours typically have to cover?

Most states that require CE tie at least part of it to NEC code updates, with some adding state-specific law or safety content on top.

Texas requires 4 hours per one-year cycle covering the NEC, Texas Electrician Law (Occupations Code Chapter 1305), the state's administrative rules under 16 TAC Chapter 73, and NFPA 70E safety content specifically, per [TDLR's continuing education page](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/electricians/elecce.htm). Virginia's 3-hour requirement is narrower and focused specifically on NEC code updates, according to [DPOR's tradesman regulations](https://www.dpor.virginia.gov/sites/default/files/boards/Tradesman/A501-27TRADEREGS.pdf). Maryland splits the difference: 5 hours per 2-year cycle tied to the NEC, NFPA-adopted codes, or the state's own licensing statute, per [COMAR 09.09.02.01](https://regs.maryland.gov/us/md/exec/comar/09.09.02.01). The pattern across states that require CE at all: code updates are almost always part of it, and state-specific law or safety content gets added on top in states with a heavier requirement.

## Does it matter whether CE is delivered online or in a live classroom?

Not for credit purposes in most states we checked, as long as the provider is board-approved, but always confirm your specific state accepts the delivery format before enrolling.

States that require CE generally care about two things: whether the provider is approved, and whether the hours and topics match what your license type requires. Texas's provider list, for example, is maintained directly by TDLR and reports completed hours electronically, regardless of whether the course itself was delivered live or as self-paced video, per [TDLR's CE page](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/electricians/elecce.htm). Maryland allows up to 5 hours of CE credit for time spent teaching a course yourself, a format option most states don't offer, according to [COMAR](https://regs.maryland.gov/us/md/exec/comar/09.09.02.01). Check with your board on format acceptance specifically if you're choosing between a live webinar and a self-paced online course. Most states are format-agnostic as long as the provider is approved, but it's worth a two-minute confirmation before you pay for a course.

## What happens if I don't complete my CE hours on time?

Consequences range from a routine reinstatement fee to a full retest, depending on the state, so check your board's specific penalty before letting a cycle lapse.

California is the strictest case: if your CE lapses along with your certificate, the 32-hour requirement itself is waived on relicensing, but you have to retake the exam for a $200 fee, a much harder reset than a late fee, per [CA DIR](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/ecu/ECU_FAQ.htm). Maryland instead uses a tiered late-fee structure: $28 within 30 days, $63 within 31 to 60 days, and $126 after that, without requiring a retest, according to [Maryland's fee schedule](https://labor.maryland.gov/license/elec/elecaff.shtml). West Virginia allows renewal without retesting for up to 5 years after a lapse, at double the standard fee, per [103CSR5](https://firemarshal.wv.gov/media/39366/download). The common thread: acting before your cycle actually expires is nearly always cheaper and simpler than reinstating after it lapses, in every state that publishes a clear penalty structure.

## FAQ

**Which states have no continuing education requirement for electricians?**
West Virginia is a clear example: its current licensing rule requires only the annual renewal fee, with no CE hours for journeyman or master electricians. Always confirm your own state directly, since rules change.

**How many CE hours do electricians need per year?**
It varies widely by state, from zero in West Virginia to an average of roughly 10-plus hours per year in California once you annualize its 32-hour, 3-year requirement. Check your specific state's board page for the exact current number.

**Do electrician CE hours have to cover the NEC?**
In most states that require CE at all, yes, at least in part. Texas, Virginia, and Maryland all tie some or all of their CE requirement specifically to NEC code content, sometimes alongside state law or safety topics.

**Can I complete electrician CE hours online?**
Usually yes, as long as the provider is approved by your state board. Most states are format-agnostic between live and self-paced online courses, but confirm with your specific board before enrolling.

**What happens if I miss my CE deadline?**
It depends on the state. Some charge a tiered late fee without requiring a retest, like Maryland. Others, like California, waive the CE requirement on relicensing but require you to retake the full exam instead.
