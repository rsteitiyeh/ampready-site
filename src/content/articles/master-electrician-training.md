Master electrician training is mostly a matter of logged field experience as a licensed journeyman, not classroom hours. States that issue a master license typically require 2 or more years working as a journeyman on top of your original apprenticeship, plus passing a master-level exam that adds business, code application, and design calculations beyond the journeyman test.

How that plays out depends entirely on where you work. Some states run a single statewide master license with a fixed hour requirement and one exam. Others have no statewide master license at all, and licensing happens city by city or county by county, each with its own rules. Figuring out which situation applies to you is the first step before any training plan makes sense.

## What does "master electrician training" actually mean?

It means logging enough supervised journeyman experience to qualify to sit for a master-level exam, plus, in some states, a short prep course covering business law and advanced calculations. It is not a classroom program you enroll in and graduate from the way an apprenticeship is.

Apprenticeship training for a journeyman license is structured: a fixed number of on-the-job-training hours paired with classroom instruction, tracked by a registered program. Master electrician training is different. Once you're a licensed journeyman, most states require you to accumulate additional years of documented field experience, often in a role with more responsibility, before you're even eligible to apply for the master exam. The "training" at that stage is the work itself, verified by supervisor sign-off, not seat time in a course.

## How does the journeyman-to-master path work?

You start as a licensed journeyman, log a set number of additional years and hours in the field, then apply to sit for a master-level exam that tests both code knowledge and, in many states, business and law topics a journeyman exam doesn't cover.

Texas is a clear worked example. The Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation requires a candidate for the master electrician license to have worked at least 2 years as a licensed journeyman electrician and to document 12,000 total hours of on-the-job training, with a $45 application fee, according to [TDLR's master electrician application page](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/electricians/apply/individuals/master-electrician.htm). That 12,000-hour figure builds on the 8,000 total hours typically needed to qualify for the journeyman license in the first place, so the master path adds roughly 4,000 more documented hours of supervised experience on top of what got you your journeyman card.

## How do requirements vary by state?

They vary a lot, and the biggest split is between states with one statewide master license and states with no statewide license at all, where cities or counties run their own separate systems.

Texas represents the first model: one state board (TDLR), one set of hour requirements, one exam vendor (PSI), one license that's valid anywhere in the state. Florida represents the second model. Florida does not issue a statewide journeyman or master electrician license. Instead, the state's Electrical Contractors' Licensing Board issues a Certified Electrical Contractor license through the Department of Business and Professional Regulation, requiring a minimum of 4 years of experience in the trade with at least 1 year in a supervisory role, tested through Pearson VUE on NEC code knowledge, Florida law, and business practices, per the [DBPR's electrical contractors licensing portal](https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/electrical-contractors/). Below that state-level contractor license, individual counties and cities, including Miami-Dade, Broward, Palm Beach, and Orange County, run their own separate journeyman and master licensing programs with their own hour requirements and exams.

Chicago is a third pattern worth knowing: a municipal license with no separate statewide Illinois electrician license backing it up. The City of Chicago requires at least 2 years of verifiable experience for its supervising electrician license (Chicago's version of a master license), tested through Continental Testing Services on a 150-question open-book exam covering electrical principles and the Chicago electrical code, with a 70% passing score, according to the [City of Chicago Department of Buildings](https://www.chicago.gov/city/en/depts/bldgs/supp_info/TLdetails/SupvEL.html). Notably, Chicago's exam is still based on the 2018 Chicago Electrical Code as of this writing, a reminder that municipal code adoption can lag well behind the national NEC schedule.

## What does the master exam typically test that the journeyman exam doesn't?

Most master exams add a business and law component and push code application further into design-level calculations, on top of everything already tested at the journeyman level.

Where journeyman exams focus on installing and troubleshooting to code, master exams in many states test whether you can run a job or a business: contracts, liability, licensing law, and sometimes basic estimating. On the technical side, expect more complex load calculations, service design questions, and code scenarios that combine multiple NEC articles into one problem, rather than single-article lookups. The national apprenticeship structure that feeds into journeyman licensing runs about 4 years with roughly 8,000 on-the-job-training hours plus 576 to 900 classroom hours, according to the U.S. Department of Labor's [apprenticeship listing for electricians](https://www.apprenticeship.gov/apprenticeship-occupations/listings?occupationCode=47-2111.00); master-level requirements build on top of that baseline rather than replacing it.

## FAQ

**Do I need to attend classes to become a master electrician?**
Usually not. Most states require documented field experience as a licensed journeyman rather than classroom hours to become eligible for the master exam. Some jurisdictions require or recommend a short business-and-law review course, but it's not a multi-year program like an apprenticeship.

**How many years does it take to go from journeyman to master?**
It depends on the state. Texas requires at least 2 years as a licensed journeyman plus 12,000 total OJT hours. Other jurisdictions set different minimums, so check your specific state or city board's current requirement before planning your timeline.

**Does every state have a master electrician license?**
No. Some states, including Florida and Illinois, have no statewide master or journeyman license. Licensing in those states happens through a state-level contractor license plus separate city or county programs, so what "master electrician" means depends entirely on where you're working.

**Is the master exam harder than the journeyman exam?**
Generally yes. It typically covers more advanced calculations and, in many states, a business and law section the journeyman exam doesn't include. The code knowledge tested tends to combine multiple articles into single scenario questions rather than isolated lookups.

**Can I skip the journeyman license and go straight to master?**
No. Every jurisdiction with a master license structure requires you to hold a journeyman license first and log additional years of experience before you're eligible to apply for the master exam.
