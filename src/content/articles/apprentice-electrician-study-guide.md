An apprentice electrician study guide, at this stage, isn't exam-cram material. There's no state licensing test for apprentices. What matters is passing an entry aptitude test if your program requires one, knowing basic circuit theory (Ohm's law, series and parallel circuits) before your first classroom module, and understanding that your progress gets tracked through logged hours and instruction grades, not a single pass/fail exam.

That's a different kind of studying than journeyman or master exam prep. You're not memorizing code articles yet. You're building the math and theory foundation the rest of the apprenticeship gets built on top of.

## Do I need to pass a test to become an apprentice?

Only some programs require a formal aptitude test, and where one exists, it's mostly algebra, not electrical knowledge.

Union programs through the Electrical Training Alliance (tied to the IBEW) and NECA use a scored aptitude test with an Algebra and Functions section, 33 questions in 46 minutes, no calculator allowed, according to [nietc.org's applicant guidance](https://www.nietc.org/applicants/preparing-to-apply/preparing-for-the-aptitude-test-and-the-interview). That's the section to actually study for before applying: solving equations, working with fractions and ratios, and basic function problems, all without a calculator. Non-union merit-shop programs through IEC or ABC chapters more commonly evaluate applicants through an interview and basic skills check rather than a standardized aptitude exam, so confirm directly with the specific program you're applying to rather than assuming a formal test applies.

## What electrical theory should I study before starting?

Ohm's law and basic series and parallel circuit behavior, since nearly everything else in year one builds on those two things.

NCCER's Electrical Level 1 curriculum, the entry module most registered apprenticeship programs use, opens with voltage, current, resistance, and power, and how those relate through Ohm's law, before moving into basic series and parallel circuit problems, per [NCCER's craft catalog](https://www.nccer.org/craft-catalog/electrical). If you walk in already comfortable with Ohm's law (V = I x R) and can explain why current splits differently in a parallel circuit than a series one, you're not memorizing new vocabulary in week one, you're applying concepts you already have a handle on. That head start compounds, since Level 2 builds AC/DC theory directly on top of it.

## What math skills actually matter at this stage?

Algebra, not trigonometry. Trig shows up later once you're doing conduit-bending calculations, but it's not part of entry-level evaluation.

Basic algebra covers most of what a new apprentice needs: solving for an unknown in a formula (rearranging Ohm's law to solve for resistance instead of voltage, for example), working with fractions and percentages, and unit conversions. If your program uses the union aptitude test, that's exactly the skill set it screens for. If you haven't touched algebra since high school, a few weeks of review before you apply or start is worth more than any electrical-specific studying at this stage.

## Is OSHA 10 required before I start?

It depends on the specific program, not a blanket national rule.

Some registered apprenticeship programs bundle OSHA 10 directly into first-year training. Monroe County Community College's NCCER-aligned Level 1 program, for example, includes OSHA 10 as part of its standard first-year curriculum, per [the college's program information](https://www.monroeccc.edu). Other programs require it separately before fieldwork starts, and some don't require it at all in year one. Check with your specific program rather than assuming it's either mandatory everywhere or unnecessary everywhere.

## How am I actually evaluated as an apprentice?

Through logged on-the-job-training hours and related-instruction grades that accumulate over the program, not a single standalone "apprentice test."

A registered apprenticeship runs about 4 years and roughly 8,000 hours of OJT, split roughly 80 to 90 percent field hours and the remainder classroom instruction (about 144 hours per year, per the federal recommendation), according to [apprenticeship.gov](https://www.apprenticeship.gov/employers/registered-apprenticeship-program). Progress and pay both track that accumulation. IBEW Local 60's published wage schedule, for example, starts a first-period apprentice at 51% of the full journeyman wage rate, stepping up through the program to 90% by the sixth period, per [the local's own wage schedule](https://www.ibewlu60.org). That step structure is standard across most union programs: your pay rises in tandem with your logged hours and instruction progress, which functions as your ongoing evaluation instead of one exam.

Nationally, registered apprenticeships across all trades grew from about 318,000 active participants in FY2014 to roughly 680,000 in FY2024, per [Community College Daily's reporting on Department of Labor data](https://www.ccdaily.com/2025/01/new-stats-on-registered-apprenticeships), and electricians make up the largest single construction trade within that pool. That growth is worth knowing if you're wondering whether apprenticeship programs are competitive to get into right now: demand on both sides, employer and applicant, has been rising for a decade.

## FAQ

**What should a new electrical apprentice study first?**
Ohm's law and basic series and parallel circuit behavior, plus algebra review if your program uses a scored aptitude test. This is the foundation NCCER's Level 1 curriculum and most first-year classroom instruction build directly on top of.

**Do I need to take a math test to become an apprentice electrician?**
Only for programs that require a formal aptitude test, most commonly union programs through the Electrical Training Alliance and NECA, which use a 33-question algebra and functions section with no calculator. Non-union programs more often evaluate through an interview instead.

**Is OSHA 10 required to start an electrical apprenticeship?**
It depends on the program. Some bundle OSHA 10 into first-year training, others require it separately, and some don't require it in year one at all. Confirm directly with your specific program.

**How is an apprentice electrician's progress evaluated?**
Through accumulated on-the-job-training hours and related-instruction grades, not a single test. Wages typically step up in tandem, starting around half of the journeyman rate and rising toward the full rate as hours and instruction accumulate.

**How long does an electrical apprenticeship take?**
A standard registered apprenticeship runs about 4 years and roughly 8,000 hours of on-the-job training, plus related classroom instruction, per federal apprenticeship standards. Some union programs run longer depending on the local sponsor.
